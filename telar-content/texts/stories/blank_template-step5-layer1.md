**The Sun**
The Sun was said to be extremely hot which made everyone sweaty, burned and tanned their skin, and gave them blisters. The Naui Olin was a festival celebrating the Sun. Celebrated every year (260 days), this involved fasting for four days before the festival day. During the festival, the indigenous people burned incense, and offered blood from the ears four times a day - sunrise, noon, past midday, and sunset. The indigenous people, in the Nahuatl text, assign a gender to the Sun - treating it like an entity with names like “The soaring eagle, the turquoise prince, the god.” There’s a constant repetition of “he” and “his”. The image shows the eclipse of the Sun and furthers this characterization. During an eclipse the Sun was described to be turning red, which is reflected in the reddish flushed expression of the Sun on the right. The only difference between the Suns in the two panels is that the eclipsing Sun on the right has “closed eyelids and a neutral expression” while normally the Sun has “vibrant, open eyes and seems to be smiling” on the left. This representation of the “passing absence of the Sun’s presence” during the eclipse reinforces the personification of the Sun. Within this we also see the effect of the European influence - the image itself is a very European depiction of the Sun with its facial characteristics . This friendly depiction in the Florentine Codex is a sharp departure from representations of the Sun before the Spanish colonization. In the Mesoamerican past, the Sun can be seen [depicted] ![](https://compositor.telar.org/objects/chapter1\_sun\_stone) (https://www.amnh.org/var/ezflow\_site/storage/images/media/amnh/images/exhibitions/permanent-exhibitions/human-origins-and-culture-halls/the-aztec-stone-of-the-sun\_smalldynamiclead/172334-1-eng-US/the-aztec-stone-of-the-sun\_smalldynamiclead\_wideexact\_2460.jpg) with the face of an “indomitable warrior, with lips parted to reveal his teeth and a sacrificial flint knife instead of a tongue” as shown in the Sun Stone image. The inverted V shapes are meant to represent the rays of the sun. The characterization and deification of the Sun done in the Nahuatl text, and the indigenous culture in general, goes against the principles of the Europeans who only had one God. This is emphasized by Sahagun in the prologue of book seven, where he dismisses the indigenous stories of the Sun, Moon, stars and more to be “ridiculous fables”. 

**What Did the People Think of the Solar Eclipse?** 
During a solar eclipse people with light complexion were slain as sacrifices. Everyone offered blood from their ears and sang in temples. The eclipse was regarded as a bad omen, with people believing that “the demons of darkness will come down” and eat men if the Sun were to be completely eclipsed.
