The stars were observed as meaningful signs that guided ritual practices and interpretations of the night. In Chapter 3 a group of stars called the Mastelejos appeared in the sky at specific times and prompted ceremonial responses. When these stars became visible, people burned incense three times throughout the night, early evening, midnight, and at dawn, to determine whether the coming night would bring prosperity or danger. These rituals show that the stars were seen as active signals that required a response. The Nahuatl text places strong emphasis on ceremony, describing offerings such as incense, blood, and ritual observances tied to the stars’ appearance. The stars are not simply described, they are personified. The comparison to a “fire drill” further reinforces this idea, as the ordered movement of the stars is like turning motion used to create fire. This connection was so significant that people marked their own bodies, burning their wrists to reflect the stars’ arrangement, demonstrating fear. Unlike the Sun, which is personified with facial features, the stars are represented differently, highlighting their collective presence rather than individuality. This difference suggests that while the Sun could be understood as a singular being, the stars functioned as a system, one that required interpretation through ritual practice.
